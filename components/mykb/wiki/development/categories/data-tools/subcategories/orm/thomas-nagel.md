## Overview

Thomas Nagel is the philosopher whose 1974 essay, What Is It Like to Be a Bat?, framed the subjective character of experience as a central problem for physicalist accounts of mind. His argument is a touchstone in the consciousness cluster, alongside [[wiki/development/categories/data-tools/subcategories/orm/david-chalmers|David Chalmers]] and [[wiki/development/categories/data-tools/subcategories/orm/consciousness-2|Consciousness 2]].

## The Bat Argument

Nagel observes that while we can know all the physical facts about a bat's echolocation, we cannot know what it is like for the bat to be a bat. The existence of such first-person facts suggests that subjective experience may not be fully captured by the objective, third-person vocabulary of the physical sciences. The argument does not claim consciousness is nonphysical, only that current reductionism leaves something unexplained.

## Influence

Nagel's framing helped separate the easy problems of consciousness — mechanisms and functions — from the hard problem of experience itself. His work anchors debates about whether a complete account of mind can be given in purely functional or informational terms. Sessions referencing Nagel typically use him to mark the boundary between what a system can do and what it is like to be the system.

## Relevance

For agent and memory research, Nagel's lesson is methodological: objective descriptions of architecture and behavior do not exhaust the phenomena of mind. Modeling exercises that borrow the vocabulary of consciousness should therefore distinguish mechanism from experience. This page keeps the reference general so it remains accurate as the surrounding theory pages grow.

Nagel's essay remains a required reference because it states the problem in the fewest possible moves: physical facts are objective, experience is subjective, and the gap between them is not obviously closed by more physics.
