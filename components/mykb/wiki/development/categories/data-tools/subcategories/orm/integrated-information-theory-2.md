## Overview

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) is a framework from neuroscience that proposes a link between consciousness and the way a system integrates information. It formalizes the idea with a quantity, Φ, meant to measure how much information the whole of a system carries beyond what its parts carry independently. High Φ is associated with rich, unified experience; systems that reduce cleanly into independent parts score lower.

## Core Ideas

- A mechanism has cause-effect power if its state constrains the past and the future of the system.
- Information is integrated when the system state cannot be decomposed into independent parts without loss.
- The framework distinguishes the easy problems of consciousness (behavior and function) from the hard problem (subjective experience), and argues that integration is the key to the latter.

## Implications for System Design

- Integration is a design property: coupling, shared state, and feedback between modules determine how much the whole exceeds its parts.
- The analogy warns against judging a system only by its parts in isolation; emergent behavior matters.
- Φ-style reasoning is qualitative in practice: engineers borrow the emphasis on integration rather than computing the quantity itself.

## Why It Appears Here

In the sessions that reference it, IIT is used as a conceptual lens for system organization — how components combine into a whole whose behavior exceeds any single part. The co-occurring tags (orm, ide, spa) suggest the idea is borrowed as an analogy rather than studied formally.
