## The Thought Experiment

The teleportation paradox asks whether a person who is scanned, destroyed, and then exactly reconstructed elsewhere is the same person who stepped into the machine. The classic setup, popularized by Star Trek's transporter and refined by Derek Parfit's teletransporter example, pits two theories of personal identity against each other:

- **Psychological continuity** — identity survives if memory, personality, and mental states are preserved, so the copy is the same person.
- **Biological and physical continuity** — identity requires an unbroken spatiotemporal history of the body, so the copy is a duplicate and the original is dead.

The paradox deepens when the original is *not* destroyed: two identical persons then exist, and it becomes unclear whether identity is a relation at all or simply continuity of a particular process.

## Why It Appears in a Technical KB

In agent and software sessions the paradox is an analogy, not a physics claim. Restoring an agent from a checkpoint, migrating a service to a new host, or replaying state from a database snapshot all raise the same question: does the reconstructed process preserve whatever made the original itself? The tags — IDE, ORM, REST, and ISR — suggest the reference arose during a discussion of state persistence and API design, where the identity of a resource across copies is decided by IDs, hashes, or versioning rather than by physical continuity.

Philosophers of mind such as David Chalmers extend the puzzle to consciousness: if the copy reports identical experiences, can we still call the original's stream of experience ended? The knowledge base records the term as an entity so future sessions can reuse the analogy when reasoning about identity and state.
