## Overview

Global Workspace Theory (GWT) is Baars's account of consciousness: at any moment, a limited amount of content is broadcast globally across a large set of specialized processors, and that broadcast constitutes the conscious experience. The page was referenced in sessions exploring the theory cluster, alongside pages such as [[wiki/development/categories/data-tools/subcategories/orm/consciousness-2|Consciousness 2]] and [[wiki/development/categories/data-tools/subcategories/orm/david-chalmers|David Chalmers]].

## Components

The theory distinguishes specialized, unconscious processors from a limited-capacity workspace. Competing processors send content into the workspace, and the winning content is broadcast back to the whole system, recruiting other processors to act on it. Attention selects what enters the workspace, which is why the theory ties attention, working memory, and conscious access together.

## Relevance to AI

GWT has been borrowed as a design metaphor for agent architectures: a global context or scratchpad that many modules read and write, with an attention-like mechanism selecting what gets broadcast. This maps naturally to transformer attention over a context window. The session references suggest the theory was used as a lens for reasoning about cognition, modeling, and the easy-versus-hard problems of consciousness recorded in the related pages.

## Criticism

Critics argue GWT explains access and reportability better than phenomenal experience itself, and that a broadcast mechanism, however elaborate, does not by itself explain why anything feels like anything. Even so, the theory remains a productive framework for experiments because it makes testable predictions about attention and report. Keeping the wiki page general preserves this balanced view.

The page is best read as a bridge between the cognitive-science literature and the modeling pages in this cluster: it supplies the vocabulary of workspace, broadcast, and attention that the related entities operationalize. Keeping the description at the level of the theory, rather than any single experiment, preserves its usefulness across sessions that approach consciousness from different angles.
