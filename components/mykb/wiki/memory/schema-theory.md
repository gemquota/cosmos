# Schema Theory

## Summary

Schema Theory — Organized knowledge structures that guide comprehension, memory, and inference.

## Details

- Schemas are structured packages of knowledge about objects, events, and situations — what a restaurant is, what a lecture looks like, how a car works. They provide slots with defaults, so comprehension fills in unstated details and memory reconstructs along schema-consistent lines.
- Bartlett's early experiments showed recall transforming toward the rememberer's cultural schemas; modern work ties schemas to faster learning (schema-consistent material is acquired more quickly) and to systematic distortions (schema-inconsistent details are lost or normalized).
- Worked example: reading 'he ordered a burger, paid, and left' activates the restaurant script, letting listeners infer the waiter, the table, and the meal without being told.
- Schema-based processing is efficient but biased: it produces top-down expectations, stereotype-consistent errors, and source confusion. Schema theory anchors cognitive load theory's claim that experts differ from novices in stored structure, not raw intelligence.
- mykb relevance: ontology and category design are explicit schema building for the knowledge graph.
