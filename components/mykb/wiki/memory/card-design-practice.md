# Card Design Practice

## Summary

Card design practice is the discipline of writing high-quality spaced-repetition cards, where each card isolates one testable fact with a clear prompt. Well-designed cards are the difference between a deck that builds durable knowledge and one that wastes review time. The practice converts raw study material into retrieval tasks that are atomic, concrete, and honest about what they ask.

## Details

- **One fact per card** — each card should test a single idea; multi-part cards create ambiguous failures and make it hard to target weak knowledge.
- **Concrete prompts** — prompts should be specific enough that the expected answer is unambiguous, avoiding vague questions that accept many answers.
- **Minimal extra cues** — formatting, position, or repeated phrasing can leak the answer, letting the reviewer succeed without real retrieval.
- **Why-connections** — linking facts to their rationale and to other knowledge makes cards more meaningful and easier to retrieve through multiple routes.
- **Card types** — basic Q&A, cloze deletion, image-occlusion, and type-in cards suit different material; the prompt style should match the task you want to practice.
- **Worked example** — instead of "What is spaced repetition?", a well-formed card asks "Why do spaced reviews outperform massed reviews?" with the answer "retrieval practice at the edge of forgetting strengthens memory traces".
- **Deck hygiene** — periodic audits that delete, split, or reword weak cards keep decks effective; failing cards may indicate a bad prompt rather than a forgotten fact.
- **Common failure modes** — overly long answers, copied textbook paragraphs, orphaned trivia, and cards that are never connected to broader concepts all reduce long-term value.
- **Practical relevance** — for self-learners using anki-style systems, card design quality often matters more than review schedule tuning, so reviewing the cards themselves is a core habit.
- **Evaluation** — a card is good if a peer can answer it correctly from prior knowledge of the topic, or if the learner can articulate why the answer matters.
