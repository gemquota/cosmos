# Counterfactual Reasoning

## Summary

Counterfactual Reasoning — Imagining alternatives to what happened, 'what if' thinking.

## Details

- Counterfactual reasoning generates alternatives to reality: what if I had studied harder, what if the patient's blood pressure had been checked. It underpins causal judgment (X caused Y if Y would not have occurred without X), blame and credit assignment, regret, and learning from experience.
- Mental simulation of alternatives is effortful and selective: people mutate the most controllable and abnormal elements of the situation, which shapes hindsight, blame, and counterfactual emotions like regret and relief.
- Worked example: after a project fails, asking 'what would have changed the outcome' identifies the pivotal decision — but only if the simulation is disciplined and evidence-checked rather than story-shaped.
- Organizational use: premortems and post-mortems are institutionalized counterfactual reasoning; both can improve decisions or merely confirm narratives, depending on rigor.
- mykb relevance: after-action-reviews and decision journals are counterfactual practice — imagining better branches to steer future ones.
