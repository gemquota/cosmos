# Bond Angle Preference

## Summary

Bond angle preference describes the favored angles between chemical bonds in molecules, explained by electron-pair repulsion and hybridization. These angles determine molecular shape, and shape determines function. The concept matters as a model of how simple repulsion rules produce structured geometry. The concept also illustrates how predictive models are built from simple assumptions about repulsion.

## Details

- **Definition** — Bond angles are the angles formed between adjacent bonds at a central atom; molecules prefer angles that minimize repulsion.
- **VSEPR theory** — Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion predicts geometry by treating electron pairs as mutually repelling regions around the nucleus.
- **Common geometries** — Two, three, four, five, and six electron domains yield linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, and octahedral arrangements.
- **Hybridization** — Orbital mixing such as sp3 produces tetrahedral geometry with angles near 109.5 degrees, while sp2 gives 120 and sp gives 180.
- **Lone pairs** — Lone pairs occupy space and compress bond angles, so water's H-O-H angle is less than the ideal tetrahedral value.
- **Worked example** — Methane has four bonds and a 109.5-degree tetrahedral angle, while ammonia's lone pair compresses its angles below that.
- **Failure modes** — Treating idealized angles as exact, and ignoring lone pairs or multiple bonds, leads to wrong structural predictions.
- **Practical relevance** — Predicting shape from simple rules is a template for modeling emergent structure throughout science and engineering.
- **Limits of VSEPR** — The theory predicts geometry well for main-group compounds but is less reliable for transition metals and excited states.
- **Experimental check** — Spectroscopic measurement confirms predicted angles, closing the loop between theory and evidence.
- **Consequences** — Angle differences drive reactivity and polarity, so geometry is not decorative.
