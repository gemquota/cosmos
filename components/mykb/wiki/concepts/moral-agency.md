# Moral Agency

## Summary
Moral agency is the capacity to act morally or be held responsible for actions. In the human case, it bundles autonomy, understanding of right and wrong, and the freedom to choose otherwise; the question for AI is whether — and in what partial sense — a system built from learned associations can be a moral agent, and what follows for blame, liability, and control.

## Details
- The criteria that define full moral agency: the agent acts intentionally (its behavior is directed by its own goals), understands the moral content of its actions (it can reason about right and wrong, not just follow rules), and could have acted otherwise (genuine choice). Current AI systems fail each criterion in the strict sense — their goals come from training, their "understanding" is behaviorally imitative, and their choices are deterministic products of weights and inputs — so they are not full moral agents on the classical account.
- AI systems currently lack full moral agency (no autonomy, no moral understanding), but partial agency is debated. The debate turns on gradation: a system that reliably weighs consequences, refuses harmful actions, and explains its reasoning is behaviorally closer to a moral agent than a system that merely follows instructions, even if neither is "autonomous" in the human sense. Some argue agency should be defined functionally — if it acts, chooses, and can be reasoned with, treat it as an agent for moral purposes — while others hold that moral agency requires capacities (understanding, freedom) that machines cannot have. The gradation matters because the more agency we attribute, the more of the action's moral weight we assign to the machine rather than its designers.
- Attributing agency affects liability, rights, and control expectations. If a system is a moral agent, it can be blamed and held responsible, which paradoxically can let designers off the hook ("the AI chose to do it"); if it is a tool, responsibility falls on the designers and operators. The stakes are asymmetric: over-attributing agency risks treating artifacts as persons, under-attributing risks hiding human responsibility behind a machine. Control expectations shift too — a moral agent has standing to refuse commands, and "the system refused" becomes a different kind of event than a malfunction.
- The safety-relevant middle ground: treat AI systems as actors with increasing moral weight without granting full personhood — the "moral patient plus accountability chain" stance — so that their decisions are scrutinized morally while responsibility stays with humans.
