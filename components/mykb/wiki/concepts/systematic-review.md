# Systematic Review

## Summary

A systematic review is a methodical synthesis of research that uses explicit, reproducible procedures to find, appraise, and combine all relevant studies on a question. It matters because it replaces cherry-picked summaries with a transparent evidence base, and it exposes gaps and biases in the literature. Systematic reviews are the foundation of evidence-based practice.

## Details

- **Definition** — A systematic review specifies eligibility criteria, search strategies, and appraisal methods in advance, then applies them uniformly.
- **Protocol** — A pre-registered protocol defines the question, databases, inclusion rules, and analysis plan, limiting post-hoc selectivity.
- **Search** — Comprehensive searching across databases, registries, and citation networks aims to find unpublished as well as published evidence.
- **Appraisal** — Each study is assessed for risk of bias, and quality judgments are reported rather than folded silently into conclusions.
- **Synthesis** — Results are combined narratively or statistically; meta-analysis pools effect sizes when studies are sufficiently similar.
- **Worked example** — A review of an intervention registers a protocol, screens two thousand records, includes forty trials, and reports pooled effects with heterogeneity.
- **Common failure modes** — Incomplete searches, outcome switching, ignoring publication bias, and combining studies that are too heterogeneous to pool.
- **Practical relevance** — Guidelines, policy, and product decisions rest on systematic reviews because they make the evidence audit trail explicit.
- **Limits** — A review inherits the quality of its included studies; systematic method makes bias visible, not absent.
- **Screening** — Titles and abstracts are screened against eligibility criteria in duplicate, with disagreements resolved by discussion or a third reviewer.
- **Data extraction** — Standardized forms capture outcomes and context from each included study, and extraction is checked for accuracy.
- **Worked example** — Two reviewers independently screen three thousand records, achieving high agreement; discrepancies are adjudicated against the protocol's inclusion rules.
- **Reporting** — PRISMA-style flow diagrams and checklists report the search, screening, and appraisal pipeline transparently.
