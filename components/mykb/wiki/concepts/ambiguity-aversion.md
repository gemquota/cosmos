# Ambiguity Aversion

## Summary

Ambiguity aversion is the tendency to prefer options with known probabilities over options whose probabilities are unknown, even when expected values are equal. It matters because it shapes real choices — insurance, investment, and novel technology adoption — and explains behaviors that expected-utility theory cannot. The classic demonstration is the Ellsberg paradox.

## Details

- **Definition** — Given two gambles with identical expected value, people systematically avoid the one with unspecified probabilities, revealing a distaste for ambiguity itself.
- **Ellsberg paradox** — Urns with known versus unknown color compositions elicit preferences that violate subjective expected utility, isolating ambiguity as a factor.
- **Mechanism** — Competing accounts cite fear of being judged, perceived control, and a general dislike of missing information.
- **Worked example** — Investors demand higher returns from ambiguous assets, and patients delay treatments whose success probabilities are poorly quantified.
- **Common failure modes** — Treating ambiguity as risk and pricing it identically, or overreacting to unknown probabilities with extreme caution.
- **Practical relevance** — Communicating uncertainty explicitly, offering staged commitments, and designing defaults reduce the drag of ambiguity aversion.
- **Variants** — Knightian uncertainty and model uncertainty are related distinctions between unknown probabilities and unknown models.
- **Limits** — Ambiguity aversion varies by domain, culture, and framing, so it is a tendency with boundaries, not a universal law.
- **Measurement** — Experimental designs vary the presence of missing probability information while holding payoffs equal to isolate ambiguity effects.
- **Domain dependence** — Aversion is stronger in unfamiliar, high-stakes, and socially evaluated domains, and weaker where people feel competent.
- **Worked example** — A consumer faces two warranties with identical coverage, one with a well-documented claims history and one without; most choose the documented one.
- **Application** — Policymakers and product teams reduce aversion by making probability information explicit and comparable across options.
