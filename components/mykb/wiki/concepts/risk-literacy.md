# Risk Literacy

## Summary
Risk literacy is the practical skill of reading risk numbers correctly: absolute versus relative risk, survival framing, and uncertainty ranges. Poor risk literacy drives bad medical, financial, and personal choices. Gigerenzer's work shows most people, and many doctors, struggle without training.

## Details
- **Key skills** — absolute vs relative risk, denominators, and framing. The classic example: a drug "reduces risk by 50%" sounds dramatic, but if the absolute risk drops from 2 in 1000 to 1 in 1000, the real effect is tiny — the relative reduction hides the small base rate. The literacy skill is always asking "percent of what?" and translating relative claims into absolute counts ("out of how many?"). Denominators matter the same way: "1 in 10" and "1 in 10,000" both sound like "one in ten thousand" is the same phrase shape, but the magnitudes differ by orders of magnitude. Framing matters because the same fact can be stated as survival or mortality ("90% survive" vs "10% die") and the two frames produce systematically different decisions.
- **Evidence** — icon arrays and natural frequencies improve comprehension. Gigerenzer and colleagues showed that presenting the same information as natural frequencies ("of 1000 women who screen positive, about 10 actually have the disease") instead of conditional probabilities ("the positive predictive value is 1%") dramatically improves both laypeople's and doctors' accuracy. Icon arrays (visual grids of 100 or 1000 people, colored by outcome) have the same effect, because they make the denominator visible. The lesson for communicators: format is not decoration, it is part of the content.
- **Consequences** — screening decisions and medication choices hinge on it. Patients who misread risk numbers make different screening, medication, and surgery decisions — overestimating screening benefits and underestimating harms (false positives, overdiagnosis), or avoiding effective treatments because a relative-risk scare was misunderstood. The same mechanics drive financial decisions (fees expressed in percentages, "guaranteed" returns without risk disclosure) and everyday technology decisions.
- The failure mode of risk communication is unintentional manipulation: statistics presented to persuade rather than inform — relative risk without absolute context, cherry-picked endpoints, uncertainty hidden behind point estimates.
- **In mykb** — risk notes should quote absolute numbers and ranges. The knowledge base's own risk documentation follows the same discipline: absolute rates, explicit denominators, and uncertainty ranges, so that a reader of the wiki inherits literacy rather than hype.
