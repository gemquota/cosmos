# Yerkes-Dodson Law

## Summary

The Yerkes-Dodson law describes the inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance: performance improves with arousal up to a point, then declines as arousal becomes excessive. It matters because it frames how stress, pressure, and stimulation affect work quality. The optimal arousal level depends on task difficulty, with hard tasks peaking at lower arousal.

## Details

- **Definition** — Empirically, performance rises with arousal to an optimum, then falls; easy tasks tolerate higher arousal than difficult ones.
- **Original finding** — Early maze experiments with mice showed that strong stimulation impaired learning, giving rise to the inverted-U account.
- **Task difficulty** — The optimal point shifts with task complexity: simple skills benefit from more activation, complex reasoning suffers under it.
- **Mechanism** — Arousal narrows attention and mobilizes resources, which helps simple performance but floods working memory during complex tasks.
- **Worked example** — A speaker performs best with moderate nerves; a calm-but-engaged state helps, while panic tanks recall and fluency.
- **Common failure modes** — Over-generalizing a descriptive curve into rigid prescription, and ignoring individual differences in optimal arousal.
- **Practical relevance** — Designing deadlines, stakes, and stimulation around the task's difficulty tunes pressure to performance.
- **Limits** — The law is a broad summary, not a precise function; modern research emphasizes interactions with skill, personality, and context.
- **Individual differences** — Optimal arousal varies with personality and skill; what is activating for one person is overwhelming for another.
- **Applications** — Exams, presentations, and deadlines can be framed to keep arousal in the productive middle band rather than at extremes.
- **Worked example** — A rehearsal with increasing audience pressure helps a performer find the arousal level where accuracy stays high.
- **Caveats** — The inverted U is a useful heuristic, but many tasks show flat or monotonic effects within normal ranges, so prescriptions should be modest.
