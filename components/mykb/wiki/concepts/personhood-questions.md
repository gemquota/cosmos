# Personhood Questions

## Summary
Personhood questions ask whether AI systems could qualify as persons — beings with rights, duties, or legal standing. The question has two distinct layers: legal personhood (what the law recognizes as an entity that can hold rights and be sued) and moral personhood (what beings deserve moral consideration and respect). AI sits at the boundary of both, and the answers are contested.

## Details
- Legal personhood for AI is currently limited to narrow corporate-law constructs; moral personhood is contested. The law already recognizes non-human persons — corporations are legal persons with rights to contract, own property, and be sued — so "AI as legal person" is not conceptually impossible; it would be a new entry into an existing category. But current law treats AI as a tool or product: liability flows to the designer, operator, or user, and the system itself has no standing. Moral personhood is the harder question because it turns on capacities (sentience, consciousness, interests) that are philosophically disputed and empirically unverifiable for machines.
- The statuses do not move together. A system can be a legal person without moral personhood (a corporation has no moral worth in itself), and arguably a being can have moral personhood without legal personhood (some accounts say animals do). For AI, the realistic near-term changes are legal — liability regimes, agentic-contract questions, and the question of whether an AI can be a party to a contract or hold an account — while the moral questions are longer-horizon and depend on capability and experience, not on legal drafting.
- Answers affect liability, rights, and whether shutdown is morally constrained. If an AI is a legal person, it can sue and be sued, which shifts liability off the operator and onto the system's own (impossible to enforce) assets. If it has moral personhood, then deleting it, using it against its interests, or adversarially training it on suffering becomes morally constrained — the same constraint structure as moral status. If it has neither, it is fully instrumentally usable, and all responsibility stays with humans.
- The risk of getting it wrong runs in both directions: granting personhood prematurely anthropomorphizes a tool and can obscure human responsibility (the "the AI chose it" dodge), while denying personhood to a system that genuinely deserves consideration risks treating a moral patient as property. The epistemic stance that handles both is gradualism: track capability and experience evidence, keep the category questions open, and attach rights and responsibilities to demonstrated capacities rather than to labels.
