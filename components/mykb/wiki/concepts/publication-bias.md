# Publication Bias

## Summary

Publication bias is the systematic tendency for studies with significant, positive, or novel results to be published while null or negative results languish unpublished. It matters because the published literature then overstates effect sizes, distorting meta-analyses and evidence-based practice. The bias is a property of the publication system, not of any individual study.

## Details

- **Definition** — Publication decisions correlate with the direction and significance of results, so the visible literature is a biased sample of all research.
- **Mechanisms** — Author self-censoring, reviewer and editor preferences, and incentives to publish positive findings all contribute.
- **Consequences** — Meta-analyses built on published studies overestimate effects, and null replications of published work appear to be failures.
- **Worked example** — Ten trials test the same drug; the two significant ones are published while eight null trials go unreported, making the drug look uniformly effective.
- **Detection** — Funnel plots, file-drawer estimates, and comparing registered trials to published ones reveal the missing mass.
- **Remedies** — Result-blind review, trial registries, preprint servers, and journals that publish null results reduce the bias.
- **Practical relevance** — Evidence review must account for publication bias or it inherits the distortion; this is a core skill in systematic review.
- **Telemetry note** — The stub pairs this with open-science and replication concepts, which this note preserves as the surrounding ecosystem.
- **File drawer** — The file-drawer problem names the unpublished null studies; its magnitude is estimated with funnel plots and fail-safe numbers.
- **Registered reports** — Journals that accept studies before results neutralize the bias by deciding publishability before outcomes exist.
- **Worked example** — A funnel plot of a literature shows asymmetry toward significant studies, prompting a search of trial registries that reveals unpublished null trials.
- **Preprints** — Preprint servers reduce gatekeeping but not author self-censorship; registries remain the structural fix.
