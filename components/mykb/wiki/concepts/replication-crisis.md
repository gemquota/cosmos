# Replication Crisis

## Summary

The replication crisis refers to the widespread finding that many published results, especially in psychology and biomedicine, fail to reproduce when re-run with the same methods. It matters because it revealed systemic weaknesses — small samples, flexible analyses, publication bias — rather than isolated fraud. The response has been methodological reform across preregistration, open data, and reporting standards.

## Details

- **Definition** — A replication attempts to repeat a study and obtain the same result; the crisis is the pattern where a substantial share of attempts fail.
- **Causes** — Low statistical power, p-hacking, researcher degrees of freedom, and publication bias inflate the published effect sizes.
- **Consequences** — Overstated effects mislead practitioners and waste resources on false leads in medicine, education, and policy.
- **Worked example** — A famous priming result fails in a multi-lab replication despite identical procedures, prompting re-examination of the original design and analysis.
- **Response** — Preregistration, registered reports, large multi-lab collaborations, and open data have become institutional answers.
- **Common failure modes** — Treating single failed replications as proof of fraud, and treating successful replications as proof of truth, both overreach.
- **Practical relevance** — Evidence consumers — including AI systems trained on literature — must account for inflated effect sizes.
- **Limits** — Replication clarifies reliability, not necessarily truth: failures can mean the original was wrong, the replication was flawed, or conditions differed.
- **Multi-lab studies** — Many-labs collaborations run one protocol across sites, distinguishing effect robustness from site-specific quirks.
- **Meta-science** — The crisis spawned meta-science: the empirical study of how science itself behaves, from methods to incentives.
- **Worked example** — A many-labs replication of a social-priming effect finds a small, non-significant aggregate effect, revising the field's confidence.
- **Balance** — Replication failures are information, not scandal: each adds evidence about when and why effects hold.
