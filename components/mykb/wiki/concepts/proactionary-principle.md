# Proactionary Principle

## Summary
The proactionary principle favors proceeding with innovation absent demonstrated severe harm, prioritizing opportunity cost. Where the precautionary principle says "do not proceed until safety is established", the proactionary principle says "do not block progress until harm is demonstrated" — the burden of proof sits on those who would restrict, and the cost of missed opportunity is counted as a real cost.

## Details
- The logic is consequentialist: innovation produces benefits — cures, capabilities, economic growth — and preventing it has costs that are just as real as the harms it might avoid. The proactionary position argues that a bias toward restriction systematically undervalues these opportunity costs, and that the correct stance is to proceed, monitor, and adapt rather than to wait for certainty that never arrives. It is the intellectual frame behind "move fast and fix things" and behind the claim that overly cautious regulation harms the very people it is meant to protect by depriving them of the benefits.
- It is the counterweight to the precautionary principle in risk governance debates. The precautionary principle, in its strong form, demands proof of safety before action and treats potential harm as a reason for restriction even when the probability is low. The two principles disagree on where the burden of proof lies: precaution puts it on the innovator, proaction puts it on the regulator. In AI policy the conflict is concrete: should a new capability be deployable until shown dangerous (proactionary), or gated until shown safe (precautionary)? The choice determines who must produce evidence, and therefore who controls the pace of deployment.
- The failure mode of the proactionary stance is asymmetry blindness: the harms of action are often visible, concentrated, and immediate, while the harms of inaction are diffuse, delayed, and easy to discount. Catastrophic risks — where a single wrong action is irreversible and civilization-scale — break the "monitor and adapt" logic entirely, because there may be no chance to adapt after the fact. A coherent proactionary position must therefore carve out the tail: proceed with reversible, monitorable innovations; gate irreversible and catastrophic ones.
- AI policy debates often reduce to calibrating between the two: which capabilities are reversible enough to proceed, which are catastrophic enough to gate, and who bears the burden of proof in each category. The calibration is not philosophical — it is an expected-value argument about the size and shape of both the opportunity and the risk.
- RSIS3 relevance: the bundle proceeds with cautious passes — a balanced middle. Passes run in sandboxes, changes are staged and bounded, and outcomes are measured before consolidation — proactionary about experimentation, precautionary about the system's irreversible state.
