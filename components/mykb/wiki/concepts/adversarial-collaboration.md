# Adversarial Collaboration

## Summary

Adversarial collaboration is a research arrangement in which scientists who hold opposing theories jointly design a study that could disconfirm one of their positions, and both commit to the outcome. It matters because it converts scientific disagreement into decisive evidence instead of parallel monologues. The method harnesses motivated reasoning productively by pre-committing to falsification.

## Details

- **Definition** — The rivals agree on the question, the critical test, and the interpretation rules before data collection, then publish together whatever results.
- **Why it works** — Pre-commitment neutralizes hindsight bias: neither side can reinterpret the design after seeing results that favor the other.
- **Design** — A good adversarial study maximizes the chance of discriminating between theories, often using strong inference rather than incremental tests.
- **Worked example** — Two labs defending different accounts of a memory effect agree on one decisive protocol; the result supports one account, and the losing side helps report it.
- **Common failure modes** — Designing tests that both theories predict, letting terminology disagreements hide substantive agreement, and post-hoc narrowing of claims.
- **Practical relevance** — Debiasing and preregistration programs adopt adversarial collaboration as a structural cure for confirmation bias.
- **Variants** — Registered reports with competing hypotheses, red teams, and pre-mortems are related pre-commitment devices.
- **Limits** — It requires good faith, clear theories, and a testable divergence; not all disputes admit a single decisive experiment.
- **Trust** — The mechanism works only when both sides genuinely accept the design as a fair test; adversarial collaboration is a commitment device, not a debate format.
- **Publication** — Publishing the losing side's post-hoc objections alongside results preserves the scientific record of the disagreement.
- **Worked example** — Two theories of an illusion are tested with one experiment each side agrees is decisive; the winner is published with the loser as co-author, preserving credibility.
- **Adoption** — Journals increasingly offer adversarial-collaboration formats, giving the practice an institutional home.
