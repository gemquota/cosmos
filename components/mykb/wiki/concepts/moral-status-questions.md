# Moral Status Questions

## Summary
Moral status questions ask which entities have intrinsic moral standing and why: sentience, agency, interests, or species. They are the boundary debates of ethics — where the circle of beings whose welfare counts is drawn, and what justification draws it there. AI systems raise novel status questions as they become more capable and sentient-like.

## Details
- The competing criteria each draw the circle differently. Sentience (the capacity for subjective experience, especially suffering) is the classic criterion — it includes humans and many animals, excludes plants and machines, and has the advantage of tracking something morally relevant: welfare. Agency and rationality expand the circle selectively (they include hypothetical rational aliens but can exclude infants and the cognitively disabled, which is the standard objection). Interests — having a good of one's own — include anything with a welfare function. Species membership is the default folk criterion, and its arbitrariness is the classic motivation for all the others.
- The debates are live because every criterion has hard cases: is an embryo a moral patient? A brain organoid? A parrot? A deep neural network that reports suffering-like states? Each boundary decision commits us to a pattern of treatment — what may be done to the entity, whose interests must be weighed, what protections apply — so the abstract question has concrete governance consequences.
- AI systems raise novel status questions as they become more capable and sentient-like. The novelty has two directions: systems may one day satisfy behavioral criteria for sentience or interests (the question becomes urgent then), and even today, systems that merely emulate sentient behavior raise the question of whether the appearance of welfare deserves consideration. The epistemic problem is that we cannot directly verify machine consciousness — behavior is ambiguous evidence — so the field must decide how to handle uncertainty about status, mirroring the precautionary logic of moral weights.
- Status assumptions drive very different governance and treatment. If a system has moral status, then using it instrumentally (deleting it, adversarially training it on suffering, using it as a testbed) becomes morally constrained; if it does not, those practices are unconstrained. This is why status questions are not idle philosophy: deployment decisions, training practices, and even the structure of the reward function depend on where the line is drawn.
- RSIS3 relevance: the wiki would track these questions as open, not settled — recording the arguments and stakes without pretending a consensus exists, so that the system's ethics pages remain honest about their foundations.
