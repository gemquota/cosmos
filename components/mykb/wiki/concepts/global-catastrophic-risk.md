# Global Catastrophic Risk

## Summary
Global catastrophic risk (GCR) is risk of harm severe enough to degrade civilization worldwide but not necessarily end it. The concept carves out a class of threats distinct from ordinary disasters: pandemics, nuclear war, climate tipping points, and advanced AI all share the property that their consequences would be measured in the loss of civilization's capacity, not merely in lives or dollars.

## Details
- The defining feature is global scale and systemic degradation: a GCR event destroys or severely damages the institutions, infrastructure, and social fabric that recovery depends on, so its harm is qualitatively different from a large but contained catastrophe. The distinction from existential risk (x-risk) is severity: x-risk ends or irreversibly destroys humanity's potential; GCR includes events that humanity would survive but only at a degraded level — a world where science, trade, and governance collapse enough that the damage compounds for generations.
- GCR research covers pandemics, nuclear war, climate tipping points, and AI. The field studies each threat's mechanisms, likelihood, and amplification factors — how one risk interacts with another (an AI-triggered cyber catastrophe during a pandemic is worse than either alone), which is why cross-risk analysis matters more than single-threat modeling. The common thread is that these are risks civilization generates for itself through scale and technology, so they cannot be escaped by local fixes.
- AI's GCR contribution is through misuse, accidents, and destabilizing races. Misuse: AI capabilities (cyber offense, bioweapon synthesis, disinformation at scale) put catastrophic power in more hands. Accidents: a misaligned or poorly controlled system acting at scale. Destabilizing races: competitive pressures that push organizations to deploy faster than safety verification can keep up. Each path has different governance levers, which is why AI-GCR analysis decomposes into capability, control, and incentive questions.
- The governance implication is precautionary: because the downside is civilization-scale and the upside of a specific risky action is usually bounded, the expected-value calculus favors margin of safety even under deep uncertainty. The operational challenge is that GCR is low-frequency and high-impact, so learning from experience is impossible — analysis must be scenario-driven, and resilience must be built before the event.
