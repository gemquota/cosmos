# Hedging Language

## Summary

Hedging language consists of words and phrases that qualify the strength of a claim, such as "likely", "possibly", "suggests", or "in some cases". Hedges communicate the degree of confidence behind a statement and reduce the risk of overclaiming. The practice matters because calibrated hedging is how uncertain knowledge is conveyed honestly in science, forecasting, and AI output. Hedges are most useful when they are specific about the kind and degree of uncertainty rather than vague qualifiers.

## Details

- **Definition** — hedges mark epistemic status: how sure the speaker is, how strong the evidence is, and how general the claim is.
- **Functions** — hedges soften categorical claims, express probability, protect against overgeneralization, and signal uncertainty appropriately.
- **Lexical forms** — modal verbs (may, could), probability adverbs (probably, rarely), and attribution phrases ("the data suggest") are common hedging devices.
- **Calibration** — good hedging matches language to actual confidence; over-hedging and under-hedging are both failures.
- **Scientific writing** — hedges are central to cautious interpretation of evidence and are expected in peer-reviewed writing.
- **AI output** — models should hedge when uncertain rather than fabricate confidence, a key behavior for trustworthy systems.
- **Worked example** — "The drug appears to reduce symptoms in some patients" is a hedged claim; "The drug cures the disease" is not.
- **Failure modes** — excessive hedging sounds evasive, while bold unhedged claims mislead; both erode credibility.
- **Practical relevance** — hedging language connects to uncertainty communication, risk literacy, and the calibration of machine-generated claims.
- **Relation to epistemic virtue** — intellectual honesty involves stating how sure one is, which hedging operationalizes in language.
- **Precision hedges** — phrases that name the source of uncertainty, such as citing a small sample, communicate more than generic qualifiers.
