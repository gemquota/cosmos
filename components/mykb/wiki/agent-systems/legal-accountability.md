# Legal Accountability for AI

## Summary
Legal accountability for AI assigns responsibility for AI-caused harm through the ordinary machinery of law — statutes, contracts, torts, and regulation — and asks who is liable, under what standard, and with what remedy. AI strains this machinery because causation is distributed across developers, deployers, users, and the model itself.

## Details
- **The liability question** — when an AI system causes harm, the law must map the harm onto a responsible party: the developer who trained the model, the deployer who ran it, or the user who directed it.
- **Causation problems** — algorithmic harms are statistical and probabilistic rather than the product of a single negligent act, which breaks traditional but-for causation tests.
- **Contract pathways** — in practice, accountability often flows through contracts: terms of service, licensing, and service-level agreements allocate risk before harm occurs.
- **Regulatory backstop** — sectoral regulation (medical devices, finance, vehicles) imposes pre-market duties and post-market monitoring that product-liability law alone does not provide.
- **Gaps while law lags** — statutes written before AI deployment leave gray zones: agency, mental state, and fault are hard to establish for automated decisions.
- **Practical posture** — deployers should treat legal accountability as a design input: document decisions, retain logs, carry the contractual allocation, and keep a human who can be reached.
- **Relationship to governance** — legal accountability is the enforceable floor beneath ethical AI governance; the two must be designed together or the ethical layer floats free of consequences.

- **Documentation as defense** — retained logs, decision records, and model cards are the evidentiary base for any later accountability proceeding; deployers who cannot say what their system did cannot defend its operation.
