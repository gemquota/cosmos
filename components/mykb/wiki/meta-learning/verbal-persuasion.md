# Verbal Persuasion

## Summary

Verbal persuasion is one of the four sources of self-efficacy: the influence of feedback, encouragement, and evaluative messages from others on beliefs about one's capabilities. It can raise or lower confidence depending on credibility, specificity, and the gap between what is said and what is experienced. Verbal persuasion matters because it is easy to deliver but only durable when paired with actual mastery experiences.

## Details

- **Definition** — verbal persuasion encompasses encouragement, feedback, coaching talk, and social evaluation that shape a person's efficacy beliefs.
- **Credibility matters** — persuasion is more influential when it comes from someone the person trusts and sees as competent about the task.
- **Realism requirement** — praise that exceeds actual performance is quickly discounted; persuasion works best when it reflects genuine, task-specific evidence.
- **Specificity** — concrete feedback ("your argument structure improved because your claims now have evidence") affects efficacy more than global praise ("great job").
- **Direction of effect** — persuasion can both build and undermine efficacy; harsh criticism, labels, and discouragement lower confidence.
- **Interaction with experience** — persuasion is most powerful early in learning, before people have accumulated their own performance evidence.
- **Worked example** — a coach telling a novice swimmer "your breathing timing was good on that lap" boosts confidence, especially when the swimmer can verify it.
- **Failure modes** — empty praise, comparison to others, and persuasion that ignores visible failure breed distrust and can backfire.
- **Practical relevance** — teachers, managers, and mentors can use credible, specific persuasion as scaffolding while designing mastery experiences that confirm it.
- **Measurement** — research studies manipulate persuasive messages and measure subsequent self-efficacy ratings on domain-specific scales.
