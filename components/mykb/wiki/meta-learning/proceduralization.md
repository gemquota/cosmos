# Proceduralization

## Summary

Proceduralization is the process by which declarative knowledge — knowing that — is converted into procedural skill — knowing how. In cognitive architectures such as ACT-R, repeated application of facts and rules compiles them into productions that run quickly and without conscious recall. Proceduralization matters because it explains the transition from halting, deliberate performance to fluent expertise.

## Details

- **Definition** — proceduralization transforms explicit, verbally accessible knowledge into condition-action rules that can fire automatically.
- **ACT-R account** — in ACT-R, declarative chunks are encoded in memory, and with practice they become embedded in production rules that match conditions and trigger actions.
- **Speed and fluency** — proceduralized knowledge executes faster and places less load on working memory than equivalent declarative recall.
- **Loss of verbal access** — the cost of proceduralization is that experts often cannot explain their skill; the knowledge is no longer stored in an explicit form.
- **The "can't explain how" phenomenon** — skilled performers describe their actions in simplified, sometimes inaccurate terms because the underlying rules are not introspectable.
- **Relation to stages** — proceduralization corresponds to the transition from the cognitive to the associative and autonomous stages of skill acquisition.
- **Worked example** — a novice grammar learner applies the rule "subject-verb agreement" deliberately; a fluent speaker just produces the correct form.
- **Failure modes** — proceduralizing flawed rules locks in errors, and over-proceduralization can make behavior rigid when context changes.
- **Countermeasures** — deliberate practice and reflective review keep procedural skill connected to explicit understanding, preserving flexibility.
- **Practical relevance** — training design should support proceduralization through practice while retaining reflective checks for adaptability.
