# Attribution Theory

## Summary

Attribution theory describes how people explain the causes of events and how those explanations shape emotion, motivation, and future behavior. In achievement settings, the key causal dimensions are locus, stability, and controllability. The theory matters because the explanation a learner gives for success or failure — not the outcome itself — often determines whether they persist or give up.

## Details

- **Definition** — attribution theory studies the perceived causes of outcomes and the consequences of those causal beliefs for affect and action.
- **Causal dimensions** — causes are classified by locus (internal or external), stability (stable or fluctuating), and controllability (within or beyond one's control).
- **Emotional consequences** — internal, stable attributions for failure (ability) produce shame and hopelessness, while unstable, controllable attributions (effort) preserve hope.
- **Motivational consequences** — attributing failure to lack of effort encourages renewed effort; attributing it to fixed ability discourages trying.
- **Explanatory style link** — the same dimensions organize explanatory style, with pessimistic patterns emphasizing stability, globality, and internality for bad events.
- **Reattribution training** — interventions teach learners to attribute failure to controllable, changeable causes such as strategy and effort.
- **Worked example** — two students fail the same test; one thinks "I didn't prepare with the right method" and studies differently, the other thinks "I'm not smart" and withdraws.
- **Failure modes** — blaming external factors for every setback prevents learning, while self-blame for uncontrollable causes creates helplessness.
- **Practical relevance** — teachers' feedback shapes attributions, so how success and failure are framed is itself an instructional decision.
- **Measurement** — attribution research uses questionnaires, vignettes, and content analysis of spontaneous explanations.
