# Explanatory Style

## Summary

Explanatory style is a person's habitual way of explaining the causes of good and bad events. It is usually described along three dimensions: permanence, pervasiveness, and personalization. The style matters because it shapes emotional reactions, persistence, and motivation: pessimistic explanations for setbacks predict helplessness, while optimistic explanations predict continued effort.

## Details

- **Definition** — explanatory style captures whether people attribute causes as stable or temporary, global or specific, and internal or external.
- **Permanence** — a pessimistic style treats bad events as permanent ("I always fail") while an optimistic style treats them as temporary ("this time went badly").
- **Pervasiveness** — pessimism generalizes a single failure across domains ("I'm bad at everything"), whereas specificity confines it to the task at hand.
- **Personalization** — the dimension of self-blame versus external attribution; internal blame for controllable causes can be adaptive, while global self-blame is corrosive.
- **Learned helplessness** — repeated failure combined with a pessimistic style produces passivity and giving up, a core finding in the helplessness literature.
- **Optimism and persistence** — an optimistic style predicts more effort after failure, better coping, and higher achievement in challenging settings.
- **Measurement** — styles are assessed through questionnaires and content analysis of spontaneous causal explanations in speech or writing.
- **Modifiability** — explanatory style is learned, and cognitive interventions can teach people to dispute pessimistic explanations and reattribute causes.
- **Worked example** — after failing an exam, a pessimistic student thinks "I'm stupid and always will be", while an optimistic peer thinks "I didn't study the right material this time".
- **Practical relevance** — the concept grounds resilience training and links attribution processes to emotion, motivation, and wellbeing outcomes.
