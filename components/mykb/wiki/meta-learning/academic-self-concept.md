# Academic Self-Concept

## Summary

Academic self-concept is the set of self-perceptions a person holds about their abilities in academic domains, such as mathematics, verbal skills, or specific subjects. It is domain-specific rather than global and is reciprocally related to achievement: performance shapes self-concept, and self-concept shapes subsequent performance. The concept matters because it influences course choice, effort, and persistence throughout education.

## Details

- **Definition** — academic self-concept is a person's appraisal of their competence in academic content areas, organized hierarchically and by domain.
- **Domain specificity** — a student can have high math self-concept and low verbal self-concept, so global measures miss what drives behavior in particular subjects.
- **Reciprocal effects** — achievement and academic self-concept mutually reinforce each other over time, forming self-fulfilling spirals of success or disengagement.
- **Social comparison** — self-concept is calibrated against peers; the big-fish-little-pond effect shows that equally able students in higher-performing groups report lower self-concept.
- **Internal comparisons** — students also compare across their own domains, which is why strong math performance can lower verbal self-concept within the same person.
- **Measurement** — validated scales capture subject-specific self-concepts, distinguishing them from general self-esteem and self-efficacy.
- **Worked example** — a student who consistently scores well in math but poorly in writing develops a high math and low writing self-concept, then avoids writing-intensive courses.
- **Failure modes** — feedback that emphasizes comparison, tracking, and fixed labels can distort academic self-concept away from actual capability.
- **Practical relevance** — supporting realistic, domain-specific self-concept through mastery experiences and norm-referenced reframing supports achievement.
- **Intervention** — programs that provide credible success and reinterpret comparative feedback can raise academic self-concept and downstream outcomes.
