# Possible Selves

## Summary

Possible selves are mental representations of who one might become: the hoped-for, feared, and expected selves that guide motivation and identity. They project the self into the future and give current behavior direction and meaning. Possible selves matter because vivid, accessible self-images influence goal pursuit, self-regulation, and the choices people make about learning and life.

## Details

- **Definition** — possible selves are future-oriented components of the self-concept, including versions of the self one hopes to become, expects to become, or fears becoming.
- **Motivational function** — hoped-for selves pull behavior forward while feared selves push behavior away, together steering effort and avoidance.
- **Balance matters** — motivation is strongest when a hoped-for self is matched by a corresponding feared self, creating both a target and a cost to avoid.
- **Plausibility** — possible selves motivate only if they feel attainable; distant or impossible selves produce little behavioral pull.
- **Identity development** — during adolescence and young adulthood, possible selves are actively explored and tested, linking identity and career development.
- **Worked example** — a student with a vivid image of themselves as a competent engineer studies deliberately, while the feared self of being unemployable adds urgency.
- **Interventions** — programs ask people to elaborate possible selves and connect them to concrete plans, which can improve persistence and outcomes.
- **Failure modes** — feared selves without hopeful counterparts can produce anxiety without action, and unconnected possible selves remain daydreams.
- **Practical relevance** — the construct connects motivation, identity, and future time perspective, informing mentoring and educational design.
- **Relation to episodic thinking** — possible selves are related to episodic future thinking, but are more self-defining and identity-laden than single imagined events.
