# Approach-Avoidance Motivation

## Summary

Approach-avoidance motivation describes two fundamental motivational systems: one oriented toward pursuing rewards and desirable outcomes, the other toward avoiding threats and undesirable outcomes. The balance between these systems shapes emotion, attention, and behavior in achievement and everyday settings. The distinction matters because approach and avoidance are not simply opposites — each has distinct antecedents, physiology, and consequences. The two systems are asymmetrical: losses and threats generally capture attention more strongly than equivalent rewards, which has consequences for how tasks are framed.

## Details

- **Definition** — approach motivation energizes movement toward positive stimuli or goals; avoidance motivation energizes movement away from negative stimuli or threats.
- **Neural systems** — the behavioral activation system supports approach and reward sensitivity, while the behavioral inhibition system supports avoidance, anxiety, and inhibition.
- **Personality dimension** — individuals differ in trait sensitivity to reward and punishment, predicting risk-taking versus caution across situations.
- **Achievement settings** — approach-oriented students seek challenge and growth, while avoidance-oriented students protect against failure, affecting task choice and persistence.
- **Affective profile** — approach is linked to hope and excitement; avoidance to anxiety, fear, and relief, coloring the experience of learning.
- **Goal framework** — achievement goal theory maps onto this distinction: mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance goals.
- **Worked example** — before a competition, an approach-motivated athlete focuses on performing well; an avoidance-motivated one focuses on not choking, which changes arousal and attention.
- **Failure modes** — chronic avoidance motivation leads to missed opportunities, withdrawal from challenge, and anxiety-driven performance.
- **Practical relevance** — understanding the two systems helps in designing environments that make rewards salient and threats proportionate.
- **Measurement** — questionnaires and behavioral tasks assess sensitivity of the approach and avoidance systems separately.
- **Negativity bias** — threats loom larger than rewards of equal magnitude, so avoidance cues can dominate attention unless environments deliberately emphasize gains.
