# Sequence Learning

## Summary

Sequence learning is the acquisition of knowledge about ordered patterns of events, actions, or stimuli. It ranges from implicit learning of statistical regularities, as in serial reaction time tasks, to deliberate practice of movement sequences in sport and music. Sequence knowledge matters because it underlies language, motor skill, prediction, and much of everyday expertise.

## Details

- **Definition** — sequence learning captures the order and dependencies between elements, so that encountering one element generates expectations about the next.
- **Implicit learning** — people can learn sequences without awareness; in serial reaction time tasks, responses speed up for patterned trials even when participants cannot report the pattern.
- **Serial reaction time task** — the classic paradigm presents stimuli in a repeating order while measuring response times, with a switch to random order revealing learned expectations.
- **Statistical structure** — sequence learning tracks transition probabilities and chunk structure, making it a form of statistical learning over time-ordered input.
- **Motor sequences** — typing, playing scales, and athletic routines are learned as ordered action patterns that become faster and more automatic with practice.
- **Language connection** — word order, morphology, and syntax are sequence phenomena; sequence learning abilities relate to language acquisition.
- **Prediction** — a core function of sequence knowledge is prediction: learned order allows anticipation of what comes next in perception and action.
- **Chunking** — practiced sequences are compressed into chunks, reducing working memory load and enabling fluent execution.
- **Failure modes** — interference, fatigue, and attention overload disrupt sequence learning; overlearning can also make sequences inflexible.
- **Practical relevance** — understanding sequence learning informs practice design, language instruction, and rehabilitation of movement skills.
