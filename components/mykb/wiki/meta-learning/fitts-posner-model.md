# Fitts-Posner Model

## Summary

The Fitts-Posner model describes skill acquisition as a progression through three stages: cognitive, associative, and autonomous. Learners begin with explicit, effortful, error-prone performance and end with fluent, automatic execution requiring little attention. The model matters because it organizes practice design and explains why expertise feels effortless: the cognitive load shifts from the task to higher-level decisions.

## Details

- **Cognitive stage** — the learner builds a declarative understanding of the task, attends to instructions, and makes frequent errors; performance is slow and conscious.
- **Associative stage** — components are refined and linked; errors decrease as the learner detects and corrects mistakes, and verbal mediation fades.
- **Autonomous stage** — execution becomes smooth and automatic; the skill runs with minimal attentional resources and can be combined with other activities.
- **Attention shift** — the stages describe the gradual freeing of working memory, connecting to cognitive load theory and automaticity research.
- **Domain generality** — the model applies to motor skills, typing, driving, and cognitive skills such as reading and problem-solving.
- **Worked example** — a new driver thinks through each pedal and mirror movement (cognitive), then drives with fewer corrections (associative), then converses while driving (autonomous).
- **Practice implications** — early practice benefits from instruction and feedback; later practice requires challenge and variability to continue improving.
- **Failure modes** — skipping the cognitive stage, or remaining forever at slow deliberate execution, prevents fluency; so does practicing only easy repetitions.
- **Practical relevance** — the model guides curriculum sequencing, coaching, and self-directed practice by identifying what kind of support each stage needs.
- **Relation to other models** — it complements proceduralization and knowledge compilation accounts of the same transition from declarative to procedural knowledge.
