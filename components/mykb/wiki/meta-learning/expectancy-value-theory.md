# Expectancy-Value Theory

## Summary

Expectancy-value theory explains motivation and achievement-related choices as the product of two beliefs: the expectation of success and the subjective value of the task. People engage with tasks they believe they can master and that they find intrinsically interesting, useful, important, or worth the cost. The theory matters because it integrates ability beliefs with valuing, predicting course choices, persistence, and performance across domains. Because the two components combine, raising motivation usually requires addressing both beliefs, not just one.

## Details

- **Definition** — motivated choice depends on expectancy (can I do this?) multiplied against subjective task value (do I want to?).
- **Expectancy component** — expectations of success draw on self-concept of ability, self-efficacy, and perceptions of task difficulty.
- **Value component** — task value has facets: intrinsic interest, utility for future goals, attainment importance to identity, and cost in effort and opportunity.
- **Costs** — effort, time, and emotional costs subtract from value; high costs can neutralize otherwise attractive tasks.
- **Developmental change** — values and expectations shift with age, experience, and social feedback, so early experiences shape later choices.
- **Domain specificity** — expectancies and values are domain-specific, explaining why a student may choose math but avoid writing courses.
- **Worked example** — a student chooses a computer science elective because they expect to do well (high expectancy) and find it interesting and career-relevant (high value).
- **Failure modes** — interventions that raise only expectancy or only value underperform; both components must be addressed together.
- **Practical relevance** — the theory guides educational design: credible success experiences, relevance framing, and cost reduction all support engagement.
- **Measurement** — validated scales separate expectancy and value components, allowing targeted diagnosis of motivational barriers.
- **Combined interventions** — programs that build genuine skill confidence while also demonstrating relevance and reducing costs outperform single-sided approaches.
