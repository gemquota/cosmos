# Achievement Emotion Theory

## Summary

Achievement emotion theory, chiefly developed by Pekrun, explains the emotions students experience in achievement settings through appraisals of control and value. Pride, anxiety, boredom, enjoyment, and shame are not side effects of learning; they shape attention, motivation, and strategy use. The theory matters because emotions feed back into achievement, making emotional regulation part of effective learning. Emotions are not merely consequences of achievement; they are causes of it, which makes emotional climate an instructional variable.

## Details

- **Definition** — achievement emotions are emotions tied to achievement activities and outcomes, such as studying, tests, success, and failure.
- **Control-value framework** — emotions arise from appraisals of control over outcomes and the subjective value of the activity or outcome.
- **Taxonomy** — emotions are classified by valence (pleasant or unpleasant) and activation (activating or deactivating), yielding groups like activating-positive (enjoyment) and deactivating-negative (boredom).
- **Activity versus outcome** — emotions can attach to the process of learning (enjoyment, boredom) or to outcomes (pride, shame, hope, anxiety).
- **Effects on learning** — activating-positive emotions generally support flexible, self-regulated learning, while deactivating-negative emotions undermine persistence.
- **Anxiety** — test anxiety is the most studied achievement emotion; it can motivate preparation but impairs performance when task-irrelevant worry consumes attention.
- **Feedback loops** — emotions influence achievement, which then confirms or changes appraisals, creating self-reinforcing cycles.
- **Worked example** — a student who values math but doubts control over outcomes feels anxious before tests, which reduces performance and strengthens the appraisal.
- **Failure modes** — chronic boredom signals a mismatch between task demands and value, and habitual anxiety can become a stable pattern.
- **Practical relevance** — instruction can shape emotions through task design, feedback, autonomy support, and direct emotion-regulation training.
- **Classroom design** — task structure, feedback style, and evaluation framing influence the appraisals that generate achievement emotions.
