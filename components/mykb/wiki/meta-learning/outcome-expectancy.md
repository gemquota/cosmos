# Outcome Expectancy

## Summary

Outcome expectancy is the belief that a behavior will produce a particular outcome or consequence. It is distinct from self-efficacy: efficacy concerns whether one can perform the behavior, while outcome expectancy concerns what the behavior will yield. Both beliefs matter for motivation, because people pursue actions they think they can do and that they expect to lead to valued results.

## Details

- **Definition** — outcome expectancy is a judgment about the causal link between an action and its results, ranging from physical to social and self-evaluative outcomes.
- **Distinction from efficacy** — a person may be confident they can exercise (high efficacy) but believe it will not help them (low outcome expectancy), so motivation requires both.
- **Role in social cognitive theory** — outcome expectancies, combined with self-efficacy, predict goal setting, effort, and persistence in bandura's framework.
- **Types of outcomes** — physical outcomes (health, comfort), social outcomes (approval, status), and self-evaluative outcomes (pride, satisfaction) each carry expectancy.
- **Expectancy-value theory** — related frameworks treat motivation as the product of success expectations and subjective task value, with outcome expectancy as a component.
- **Worked example** — a student studies because they expect studying to raise their grade, and they value high grades; weaken either belief and motivation drops.
- **Formation** — expectancies develop from experience, observation of others, persuasion, and cultural messages about what actions achieve.
- **Failure modes** — cynical beliefs that effort is futile, or overoptimistic expectations that ignore real costs, both distort decision-making.
- **Practical relevance** — interventions can target outcome expectancy by providing credible evidence that effort pays off, alongside building efficacy.
- **Measurement** — expectancies are assessed with questionnaires asking how likely specific outcomes are if the person performs the behavior.
