# Power Law of Practice

## Summary

Power Law of Practice — The regular finding that performance improves as a power function of practice.

## Details

- The power law of practice (Newell & Rosenbloom) states that response time or error rate decreases as a power function of the number of practice trials: T = a × N^b. Improvement is rapid at first and slows with practice, with b typically between -0.5 and -1 across many tasks.
- The law holds across domains — typing, motor skills, mental arithmetic, reading — and motivated cognitive architectures like ACT-R, where it emerges from chunking and production strengthening. Log-log plots of performance versus trials approximate straight lines.
- Worked example: 100 trials cut task time by half; the next 100 trials cut it by a quarter of that — improvement per trial falls, which is why the later stages of expertise require deliberate, not naive, practice.
- Interpretation cautions: the law describes trends, not rigid determinism; performance can step-change with new strategies, and aggregation across learners can create spurious power curves.
- mykb relevance: learning-curves and deliberate-practice entries show what the law implies for training design — early mass practice, later targeted practice.
